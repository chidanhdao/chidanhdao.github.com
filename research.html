Rewards, Credit Access, and Inequality in the Credit Card Market

Job Market Paper, 2026

Abstract: This paper examines the distributional consequences of credit cards by quantifying how unevenly welfare gains from these products are distributed across consumers. Whereas existing literature models credit cards as either payment methods or sources of liquidity; I model demand for both functions and quantify their value to consumers. I first show that cardholders who borrow from their credit cards (i.e., Revolvers), do so to fund their consumption. Because nominal reward-based metrics (i.e., rewards minus fees) omit this consumption utility, they understate the benefits of credit cards to these consumers. This omission is consequential: accounting for both functions reveals that credit cards generate CAD 9.1 billion in annual welfare gains to consumers, far exceeding the CAD 0.825 billion implied by reward-based metrics. Because revolving cardholders often receive negative net monetary transfers and earn lower incomes than their non-revolving counterparts, reward-based metrics also overstate the Gini coefficient of welfare gains by 44\%. Finally, eliminating the credit function increases the Gini coefficient of card-derived welfare gains by 7\%. This result indicates that access to revolving credit can be progressive and challenges the view of credit card borrowing as inherently exploitative.

Profiles of Debt: Revolving Behavior and Financial Fragility

Abstract: This paper examines the financial well-being and consumption dynamics of credit card users who carry revolving balances (i.e., Revolvers). Using microdata from the Bank of Canada’s Digital Wallet and Payment Trends survey, I find limited evidence that Revolvers lack sufficient savings to absorb unexpected shocks and maintain adequate consumption during retirement. Despite earning less and appearing to face tighter liquidity constraints, Revolvers report financial assets, liabilities, and financial comfort similar to non-revolving consumers (i.e., Convenience users). Available evidence also undermines concerns that revolving cardholders experience sharp declines in consumption during retirement. While Revolvers' consumption levels are slightly lower than those of Convenience users near retirement, this reflects broader differences in life-cycle patterns between groups of cardholders rather than insufficient retirement readiness among revolving consumers. Specifically, Revolvers consume more early in life but experience slower consumption growth during their prime working years, resulting in a more compressed hump-shaped consumption profile. Taken together, these findings suggest that revolving behavior is more closely associated with liquidity needs than with the forms of financial fragility considered here..
